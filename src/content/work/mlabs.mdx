After years of studying, it grates on me to remember doing or learning something
and not be able to get it back. So I built a vault to hold everything I know.
On top of it a company has grown — a way of working I intend to keep refining
for my whole working life, storing what I do and learn **so that every project
starts from the experience of the ones before it.**

A company of one, whose employees are agents.

**→ [MLabs on GitHub](https://github.com/Marc-Recio-Celda/MLabs)**

## The method, in practice

<SessionLoop />

**Skills and roles standardise the procedures.** A procedure written once and
followed costs less than a rule held in mind every turn — and what makes a role
a role is accountability, not capability.

## What keeps it from collapsing as it grows

Every rule names the clause it serves, and coverage is regenerated from the
rules themselves — so a stated priority nothing implements shows up as a number.

<ClauseGrid />

**A role's retirement criterion is written before its first run** and kept out of its own brief: a role that knows what
retires it has a reason to manufacture what it is counted on. And **a check is
not adopted until a planted fault has fired it** — an unverified check is worse
than none, because it reports success either way.

Underneath both, **write access is gated on an agent's *initiative*, not its
hands:** what it concludes on its own waits in a mailbox for me.

## Under construction, and whether it works for anyone else

The honest status, and I would not want another: a system that stops changing
has stopped being used. **Declared rather than discovered:** no check runs yet
without a human launching it, and the repository says so before it says anything
else.

The rules were published because **if they only work with my content
inside them, they are not a method.** Someone from outside the field is using
it now in exchange for feedback — which turns *does this generalise?* into a
question being answered rather than one I assert.
